Findings

The census's six headline findings, in the order of the measurement layers, followed by the scorecard for the three expectations recorded in the preregistration. Every quantity describes the configuration of published research relative to modelled burden estimates; none ranks diseases by clinical importance.

1. The pool grew fivefold and rotated towards oncology

The 14-area pool grew from 26,544 publications in 1995–99 to 128,117 in 2020–24. The five head-and-neck cancers jointly rose from 32.1% to 44.6% of the pool, driven by nasopharyngeal cancer (8.3% → 13.4%), oral cancer (4.5% → 8.9%) and thyroid cancer (10.1% → 12.7%). Laryngeal cancer moved against its own group (3.2% → 1.4%): the rotation is within oncology as much as towards it.

2. Otitis media shrank in share, not in output

Its pool share fell from 8.4% to 2.9% while its five-year output rose from 2,229 to 3,706 publications — a textbook denominator effect, in which a still-growing area is diluted by faster-growing neighbours.

3. Hearing loss is the central level finding

In 2020–24 hearing loss carried 72.7% of the seven-cause DALY pool against 26.3% of the same pool's publications — roughly one-third of the publication share its burden share would imply under proportionality. Its log2 ABR deepened from −1.05 to −1.47 (ABR 0.48 → 0.36), the only cause below parity in the first period that ended further below it. Laryngeal cancer joined it from above (−0.18 → −1.18). At the other end, thyroid cancer held the pool's largest excess throughout (+3.05 → +3.24; ABR ≈ 9.45 in the last period).

4. Geography converged in direction, not in level

The distance between the DALY-weighted burden centroid and the first-author research centroid fell for all seven burden-tier causes (−39% to −80% between the first and last periods). Last-period distances remain large in absolute terms — 1,102 to 5,242 km: the research centre of every cause still sits on a different part of the map from its burden centre. The paths are not all smooth: nasopharyngeal cancer's distance fell to 978 km in 2015–19 and then rebounded to 2,467 km. Detail on the Geography page.

5. The authorship base broadened

The low- and middle-income country (LMIC) share of first authors across the pooled areas rose from 7.9% in 1995–99 to 39.8% in 2020–24. China accounts for much of the rise (2.5% → 21.9% of all parsed records) but not all of it: excluding China, the LMIC share of all parsed records still rose from 5.4% to 17.9%.

6. Typology: two robust types, one stable misalignment, three unclassified

Under the rule-based decoupling typology, otitis media is resolution-dilution (a relative share dilution only — annualised absolute DALYs rose for all seven causes). Nasopharyngeal and oral cancer are technology-ignited, the only two assignments unchanged under all three threshold sets. Hearing loss is typed constant on dynamics only, and must be read together with its level — ABR 0.36 in 2020–24, the pool's deepest deficit: a stable misalignment, not an absence of one. Laryngeal, other pharyngeal and thyroid cancer match no rule under the main thresholds and are reported as unclassified, not forced into a type. Detail on the Typology page.

CRS/polyps. Terminology caveat: under a pre-frozen backup query built only from modern post-consensus terms, the CRS share change reverses (+251.0% instead of −26.6%). The apparent decline is read as terminology migration, not as a change in research volume, and is not carried as a headline conclusion. The CRS share decline is therefore not counted among the headline findings above.

(3) Share gains will concentrate in diseases marked by treatment or technology shifts. Half matched The head-and-neck cancers fit the expectation. Obstructive sleep apnoea, the clearest technology-associated area, rose once and then plateaued, its yearly share sitting at roughly 12–13% in every year since 2005. The CRS point originally attached to this expectation is now read as terminology migration (caveat above), not as evidence about the biologics era either way.

A scorecard of one full, one largely failed and one half is what a preregistered descriptive study should expect to publish; the failed expectation (2) is the more informative result, because it rules out the simplest version of the "research ignores the shifting burden" narrative for this specialty.
